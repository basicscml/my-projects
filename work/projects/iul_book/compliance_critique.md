## 1. Top compliance risks, in priority order

### R1 — The word "Bulletproof" itself (Title; §3) — HIGHEST
A superlative title promising safety is the single biggest exposure. State
insurance advertising rules (most modeled on the NAIC Life Insurance
Illustrations Model Regulation and unfair-trade-practices acts) prohibit ads
that are misleading or that imply guarantees the product doesn't provide. "IUL"
is not principal-guaranteed in the way "bulletproof" implies — fees can reduce
cash value even in a 0% floor year.
- **Fix:** Keep the brand title but add an early, prominent definition (§3
  already starts this — make it page 1, not chapter 3) stating exactly which
  risks are reduced and which remain. Add a cover/inside-cover disclaimer.
- **Review:** YES — title/marketing claims must clear compliance.

### R2 — "Tax-free income" framing of policy loans (§8, §9) — HIGH
Describing IUL loan distributions as "tax-free income" is one of the most
heavily scrutinized IUL claims. Loans are not income; the tax treatment depends
on the policy staying in force. A lapsed or surrendered over-loaned policy can
trigger a large taxable event, and a MEC is taxed differently.
- **Fix:** Never say "tax-free." Say "generally income-tax-advantaged when
  structured and maintained properly," always paired with the lapse/MEC warning.
  §10 and §11 must be cross-referenced here, not separated.
- **Review:** YES — tax statements need legal/tax review; add "consult a tax
  advisor."

### R3 — Illustrated (non-guaranteed) rates read as expectations (§5, §6, §11) — HIGH
Caps, participation rates, and spreads are **non-guaranteed and can change**.
The outline acknowledges this in §11, but §3's "bulletproof" promise and §5's
crediting mechanics can leave a reader anchoring on illustrated returns.
- **Fix:** Every illustration discussion must show guaranteed vs. non-guaranteed
  columns and state plainly that the insurer can change caps/par rates/spreads.
  Avoid any single "expected return" number.
- **Review:** YES — illustration language is directly regulated.

### R4 — Education vs. lead-generation ambiguity (Open Questions) — HIGH
The outline itself flags this, correctly. If the book is lead-gen for a practice,
it becomes **advertising** and triggers agent licensing disclosure, "this is a
solicitation," and state-specific ad-filing rules. If purely educational, the bar
is lower but misrepresentation rules still apply.
- **Fix:** Decide before drafting. If lead-gen, add solicitation disclosure +
  agent name/license + state availability.
- **Review:** YES — this decision sets the entire disclosure regime.

### R5 — The 0% floor "protects you" claim (§6) — MEDIUM-HIGH
A floor protects the *index credit* from being negative; it does **not** protect
cash value, because cost of insurance, admin fees, and rider charges are still
deducted in a flat year. Readers routinely misread "0% floor" as "can't lose
money."
- **Fix:** State explicitly: "A 0% floor means the index credit won't be
  negative, but policy charges can still reduce your cash value in a flat year."
  Tie directly to §7 (costs).
- **Review:** Recommended.

### R6 — Naming carriers/products (Open Questions) — MEDIUM (conditional)
If specific products are named, the compliance bar rises sharply: claims must
match the carrier-approved material, and many carriers require their own
advertising review/approval of third-party content mentioning them.
- **Fix:** Prefer generic/educational treatment. If naming products, route
  through each carrier's advertising-review process.
- **Review:** YES if any product is named.

### R7 — Comparisons stated as universal truths (§12–§14) — MEDIUM
"IUL vs. 401(k)/IRA/term/whole life/annuity" comparisons are fine as education
but risky if framed as "IUL wins." Disparaging other products or making blanket
suitability claims invites unfair-trade-practice scrutiny.
- **Fix:** Frame as "different jobs, different trade-offs" (§12 already does this
  — apply it consistently to §13–§14). Avoid "better than" verdicts; use
  suitability-by-situation language.
- **Review:** Recommended.

### R8 — Missing "not a bank/securities product" distinctions (Part II) — MEDIUM
Readers conflate IUL cash value with investing in the index and with insured
deposits. Absent clear statements, this is a misrepresentation risk.
- **Fix:** State plainly: you are **not** invested in the index; there are **no**
  dividends; it is **not** FDIC-insured; it is **not** a security/investment
  account.
- **Review:** Recommended.

## 3. Must-have disclaimers for a consumer IUL book

1. Educational only; not financial, tax, legal, or investment advice.
2. Not an offer or solicitation to buy insurance (unless intentionally lead-gen,
   in which case the opposite disclosure + license info is required).
3. IUL is a life insurance product, not an investment; cash value is not invested
   in any index and earns no dividends.
4. Not FDIC-insured; not a bank or securities product; not guaranteed by any
   government agency.
5. Caps, participation rates, and spreads are set by the insurer and **can
   change**; illustrated values are not guarantees of future results.
6. Policy charges (cost of insurance, fees, surrender charges) reduce value and
   can cause lapse if the policy is underfunded or over-loaned.
7. Tax treatment depends on the policy remaining in force and on MEC status;
   consult a qualified tax advisor.
8. Product availability, features, and rules vary by state and by carrier.
9. Consult a licensed insurance professional before purchasing.

## 4. Structural gaps to add

- **A dedicated "What can go wrong" chapter.** §10 touches lapse/MEC, but a
  standalone worst-case chapter (lapse, over-loaning, underfunding, rising cost
  of insurance at older ages) protects both reader and author and strengthens
  the honest-broker positioning.
- **A "How illustrations can mislead" section** beyond §11 — show a side-by-side
  of guaranteed vs. illustrated so the reader internalizes the gap.
- **A standing disclosure block** repeated at the start of Parts III and IV
  (where the tax and comparison risks concentrate), not just once up front.
- **Sources/methodology note** for any figures used, so claims are traceable.
- **A reviewer sign-off page** (internal) tracking compliance/legal approval
  before publication.

### Bottom line
The outline is honest in instinct (it already flags illustrations, lapse, MEC,
and the lead-gen question) — that's a strong foundation. The concentrated risk is
in **language**: "bulletproof," "tax-free," and any phrasing that turns
non-guaranteed mechanics into expectations. Fix the language, front-load the
disclosures, add a "what can go wrong" chapter, and resolve the
education-vs-lead-gen question before drafting prose. None of this is publishable
until a licensed insurance-compliance reviewer for your target state(s) signs off.
